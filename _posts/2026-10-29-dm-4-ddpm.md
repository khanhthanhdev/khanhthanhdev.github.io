For five years after the 2015 paper, diffusion models were a curiosity. They had a clean theory and a tractable bound, but their samples were not in the same league as GANs. Then in 2020 Jonathan Ho, Ajay Jain and Pieter Abbeel published [_Denoising diffusion probabilistic models_](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11239) (DDPM). Their unconditional CIFAR-10 model reached an FID of 3.17, better than most GANs of the time, including class-conditional ones.

They didn't change the forward process, and they didn't add a new kind of network. They changed two things: **what the network predicts**, and **how the loss weighs the steps**. Both changes are a few lines of algebra on the bound from [part 3]({% post_url 2026-10-29-dm-3-reverse-process %}), and together they turned training into the simplest regression imaginable: show the network a noisy point and ask it what noise was added.

This part does that algebra, then lets you train the network yourself, then reruns the paper's key comparison on the toy spiral.

<div class="tf-step" data-step="three" data-title="Three things a network could predict." markdown="1">
**Here is one training example**, zoomed in: a clean point $$x_0$$, the noise $$\varepsilon$$ drawn for it, and the noisy point at step 40,

$$x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$$

The red arrow is the noise part. The green dot is the forward posterior's centre $$\tilde\mu_t$$ from part 3, the target the bound asks the network to hit.

Given $$x_t$$ and $$t$$, knowing any one of $$\tilde\mu_t$$, $$x_0$$ or $$\varepsilon$$ gives the other two: the formula above links $$x_0$$ and $$\varepsilon$$, and $$\tilde\mu_t$$ is a weighted mix of $$x_0$$ and $$x_t$$. So a network could output any of the three. The 2015 paper predicted the centre. DDPM predicts the **noise**.

</div>

<div class="tf-step" data-step="weights" data-title="The bound, written in the noise." markdown="1">
**Substitute.** Write $$x_0$$ in terms of $$x_t$$ and $$\varepsilon$$, put it into $$\tilde\mu_t$$, and the posterior centre becomes

$$\tilde\mu_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon\Big)$$

So DDPM gives the model's centre the same form, with the network's guess $$\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$$ in place of the true noise. Then the bound's term at step $$t$$ becomes a squared error on the noise:

$$L_{t-1} - C_t = \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\,\alpha_t\,(1-\bar\alpha_t)}\;\mathbb{E}\big\lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\big\rVert^2$$

The green curve is that weight for our 200 steps (with $$\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$$, rescaled to average 1). It puts the most weight on the first steps, where the noise is tiny.

DDPM's second change is to **throw the weight away**, the dashed line:

$$L_\text{simple} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,x_0,\,\varepsilon}\big\lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\; t\big)\big\rVert^2$$

</div>

<div class="tf-step" data-step="train" data-title="Train one yourself." markdown="1">
**Press _train_.** A fresh network with random weights learns from scratch with $$L_\text{simple}$$, right here in your browser: Algorithm 1 of the paper, repeated 4,000 times on batches of 128.

**Algorithm 1 (training)**: repeat:

1. take a data point $$x_0$$, a step $$t$$ uniform in $$1, \dots, T$$, and noise $$\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$;
2. take a gradient step on $$\lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon, t)\rVert^2$$.

The chart is the loss. It never gets near 0: even a perfect network can't know the exact noise, only its average given $$x_t$$. The dashed line is the lowest possible loss, 0.456, computed with the exact model. Every 500 steps, the plot shows 400 samples from the network so far. It takes under a minute on a laptop, and ends with a recognisable but blurry spiral; the network shipped with these pages trained for 30,000 steps on batches twice as big.

Switch the menu to _predict x₀_ to train a network that outputs the clean point instead.

</div>

<div class="tf-step" data-step="sample" data-title="Sampling: Algorithm 2." markdown="1">
**Sampling uses the predicted noise to take each reverse step.** This is the network shipped with the page, trained for 30,000 steps.

**Algorithm 2 (sampling)**: draw $$x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$; for $$t = T, \dots, 1$$:

$$x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\Big) + \sigma_t z, \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

with no noise added at the last step. Scroll to go from $$t = 200$$ to $$t = 1$$; the strip writes out the update for one point.

Read the formula as: remove a little of the predicted noise, rescale, and add back a little fresh noise. Ho et al. pointed out that this is the shape of **Langevin dynamics**, a way to sample from a density by following its gradient with noise, with $$\varepsilon_\theta$$ playing the role of the gradient. Part 5 makes that exact.

On the spiral, the noise-predicting network gives the cleaner samples. With 4,000 samples each, measured against the true spiral, the noise-predicting network sits at 0.016, as close as 4,000 real points (0.019), and the $$x_0$$ network at 0.056. **And compare how to weigh the steps.** Both networks predict $$\varepsilon$$. The left one uses $$L_\text{simple}$$; the right one uses the bound's weights from step 2.

In the paper, this was the decisive change. On CIFAR-10, predicting $$\varepsilon$$ with the true bound gave an FID of 13.51; with $$L_\text{simple}$$, 3.17. The bound is the objective to use if you want the best likelihood (it gave a better bound: 3.70 bits per dimension against 3.75), but flattening the weights makes better samples.

## The whole thing in a few lines

DDPM fixes the forward process (linear schedule, $$T = 1000$$) and the reverse widths ($$\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$$), and parameterises the reverse centre through a noise prediction:

$$\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\Big)$$

Training is regression on the noise, with every step weighted equally:

$$L_\text{simple} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}\{1..T\},\; x_0,\; \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)}\Big[\big\lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\; t)\big\rVert^2\Big]$$

and sampling is Algorithm 2. The paper's ablation on CIFAR-10 (Table 2, FID, lower is better):

{: .tf-table}
| network predicts | trained on | FID |
| --- | --- | --- |
| centre $$\tilde\mu$$, learned variance | true bound | 23.69 |
| centre $$\tilde\mu$$, fixed variance | true bound | 13.22 |
| centre $$\tilde\mu$$, fixed variance | unweighted MSE | unstable |
| noise $$\varepsilon$$, learned variance | true bound | unstable |
| noise $$\varepsilon$$, fixed variance | true bound | 13.51 |
| noise $$\varepsilon$$, fixed variance | $$L_\text{simple}$$ | **3.17** |

Predicting the noise is no better than predicting the centre on the true bound (13.51 against 13.22). It is the combination, noise prediction with flat weights, that works.

For scale: the CIFAR-10 network was a U-Net with 35.7 million parameters (ours has 5,714), trained for 800,000 steps at batch size 128. Our 30,000-step toy takes about four minutes on one CPU core.

## Going deeper

### What the network learns: the average noise

The minimiser of a squared error is a conditional mean. The best possible $$\varepsilon_\theta$$ is

$$\varepsilon^*(x_t, t) = \mathbb{E}\big[\varepsilon \mid x_t\big]$$

the average of all the noises that could have produced $$x_t$$, over all the $$x_0$$ it could have come from. That's why the loss has a floor: the actual $$\varepsilon$$ scatters around that average. On our toy the floor is 0.456 (computed with the exact model, averaged over all steps). The shipped network's training loss ended at 0.462.

Since $$x_0$$ and $$\varepsilon$$ determine each other given $$x_t$$, the same network also gives the best guess of the clean point, $$\hat{x}_0 = \big(x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_\theta\big)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$$. A diffusion model is a **denoiser**: give it a noisy point, and it tells you its best guess of what was underneath. Everything else is bookkeeping around that.

### Why predicting the noise helps {#targets}

All three targets are interchangeable, so the difference between them is only in how an unweighted squared error weighs the steps. Write the error on each in terms of the noise error:

$$\lVert x_0 - \hat x_0 \rVert^2 = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_t}{\bar\alpha_t}\,\lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta \rVert^2 = \frac{1}{\mathrm{SNR}(t)}\,\lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta \rVert^2$$

So an unweighted loss on $$x_0$$ is the $$\varepsilon$$ loss weighted by $$1/\mathrm{SNR}$$, which on our schedule runs from about $$5 \times 10^{-4}$$ at $$t = 1$$ to $$3 \times 10^{4}$$ at $$t = 200$$. It pours almost all of the training signal into the noisiest steps, where any guess of $$x_0$$ is poor, and almost none into the last steps that fix the detail. The $$\varepsilon$$ loss weighs every step equally on a quantity that always has unit scale.

There is also a numerical reason. The network's output always has the same scale (standard normal noise), whatever $$t$$ is, which is easy for a network to produce. Part 11 turns this into a design rule, choosing the target so the network's input and output always have unit variance, and finds that a mix of the two targets is best.

### Why the flat weights help {#why-simple}

The bound's weight on $$\lVert\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\rVert^2$$ is $$\beta_t / (2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t))$$ with $$\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$$. At $$t = 1$$ the denominator is tiny, $$1 - \bar\alpha_1 = \beta_1$$, so the weight there is about $$1/2$$; later it falls to about $$\beta_t/2$$. On DDPM's 1,000-step schedule the weight runs from 0.5 at the first step down to 0.005, a factor of about 100, and the heaviest weights sit on the first steps, whose noise is smaller than one pixel's quantisation step.

Those first steps matter for likelihood: they decide the exact pixel values, and the likelihood counts every bit. They hardly matter for how a picture looks. $$L_\text{simple}$$ lowers their share, so the network spends more of its capacity on the larger-noise steps that decide shapes and textures. In Ho et al.'s words, it is beneficial to down-weight the small-$$t$$ terms "so that the network can focus on more difficult denoising tasks at larger $$t$$ terms".

The same split showed up in the results. DDPM's samples were the best of any likelihood-based model, but its likelihood (3.75 bits per dimension) was not competitive with autoregressive models (2.80 for the Sparse Transformer). The paper's reading: most of the bits go into imperceptible detail, and diffusion models are excellent _lossy_ compressors. Latent diffusion (part 10) builds a whole architecture on that observation.

### The connection to score matching

Ho et al.'s other contribution was to notice that with the $$\varepsilon$$ parameterisation, the bound is the same objective as **denoising score matching** over many noise levels, the method of Song and Ermon's 2019 noise-conditional score networks, and Algorithm 2 is the same shape as their **annealed Langevin dynamics** sampler. The precise statement is

$$\nabla_{x_t} \log q(x_t) = -\frac{\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t]}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}$$

so a network that predicts the average noise is a network that knows the gradient of the log density of the noisy data, scaled. Part 5 starts from that gradient and builds the same model from the other direction.

### What else was in the network

The paper's network is a U-Net (a convolutional encoder–decoder with skip connections) based on PixelCNN++, with group normalisation, self-attention at 16 × 16, and the step $$t$$ fed in through a sinusoidal embedding added to every residual block. Data is scaled to $$[-1, 1]$$. Part 8 takes the architecture apart, along with the changes OpenAI made a year later.
