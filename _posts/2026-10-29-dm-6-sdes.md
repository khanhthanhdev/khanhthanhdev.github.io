Parts 1 to 4 and part 5 built two models that look different. DDPM shrinks the data while adding noise, over 1,000 numbered steps, and samples with one Gaussian step per level. The score model of Song and Ermon only adds noise, over 10 levels, and samples with 100 Langevin steps per level. Both train a network to denoise. The end of part 5 showed that DDPM's network _is_ a score network.

[Song, Sohl-Dickstein, Kingma, Kumar, Ermon and Poole (2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13456) finished the job. Let the number of steps go to infinity, and both chains become **stochastic differential equations** (SDEs): equations for a point that drifts and jiggles continuously in time. DDPM becomes one SDE, the score model another, and a 1982 result of Brian Anderson says that any such SDE can be run backwards if you know the score. That turns sampling into solving an equation, with all of numerical analysis available to do it.

This part builds that picture. No measure theory: an SDE here is just the limit of the steps we already know, with the step size shrinking. The network driving every sampler on this page is the one from parts 1 to 4, never retrained.

<div class="tf-step" data-step="walk" data-title="Smaller and smaller steps." markdown="1">
**Start with a random walk** that lasts one second. Cut it into $$n$$ steps; each step adds independent noise $$\sqrt{1/n}\,z$$, with $$z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$. Variances add, so after one second the total noise has variance $$n \cdot 1/n = 1$$, whatever $$n$$ is. The grey cloud is 600 endpoints.

Scroll to cut the same six walks into 2, 4, … up to 256 steps. The paths get rougher, but they converge to a definite random curve: **Brownian motion**, written $$w(t)$$. Its increments over a short time $$dt$$ are $$dw \sim \mathcal{N}(0, dt\, I)$$.

An SDE is a drift plus Brownian noise:

$$dx = f(x, t)\, dt + g(t)\, dw$$

move by $$f\,dt$$, and jiggle by $$g$$ times a Brownian increment. To simulate one, take small steps of exactly that: the **Euler–Maruyama** method.

</div>

<div class="tf-step" data-step="vp" data-title="DDPM's chain is an SDE." markdown="1">
**DDPM's forward step**, $$x_i = \sqrt{1-\beta_i}\,x_{i-1} + \sqrt{\beta_i}\,z$$, has exactly that form. Write $$\beta_i = \beta(t)\,\Delta t$$ with $$t = i/N$$ and $$\Delta t = 1/N$$. For small steps $$\sqrt{1 - \beta(t)\Delta t} \approx 1 - \tfrac12\beta(t)\Delta t$$, so

$$x_i - x_{i-1} \approx -\tfrac12\beta(t)\,x_{i-1}\,\Delta t + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,\sqrt{\Delta t}\,z$$

In the limit, the **variance-preserving (VP) SDE**: $$dx = -\tfrac12\beta(t)\,x\,dt + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,dw$$.

DDPM's linear schedule becomes $$\beta(t) = 0.1 + 19.9\,t$$ on $$t \in [0, 1]$$, the setting Song et al. use; our 200-step toy schedule is the same line. The figure overlays the 200-step chain (blue) and the SDE simulated with 1,000 finer steps (orange). Both are as close to the exact noised data as a sample can be.

</div>

<div class="tf-step" data-step="ve" data-title="The score model's ladder is an SDE too." markdown="1">
**Song and Ermon's noise ladder** adds noise without shrinking: $$x_i = x_{i-1} + \sqrt{\sigma_i^2 - \sigma_{i-1}^2}\,z$$. In the limit of many levels, it becomes the **variance-exploding (VE) SDE**:

$$dx = \sqrt{\frac{d[\sigma^2(t)]}{dt}}\; dw$$

no drift at all, just noise at a rate that grows with $$\sigma(t)$$. With a geometric ladder, $$\sigma(t) = \sigma_\text{min}(\sigma_\text{max}/\sigma_\text{min})^t$$.

Scroll to run both on the spiral. The VP cloud stays the same size and fills with noise. The VE cloud keeps the spiral at the same scale and buries it in noise that grows to $$\sigma = 5$$ (the right panel zooms out to fit). Same idea, different bookkeeping: part 11 shows that a simple change of variables turns one into the other.

</div>

<div class="tf-step" data-step="drift" data-title="Running an SDE backwards." markdown="1">
**Now the key result.** Anderson (1982) showed that the reverse of a diffusion is another diffusion. If $$x(t)$$ follows $$dx = f\,dt + g\,dw$$, then running time backwards, from $$t = 1$$ to $$0$$, it follows

$$dx = \big[f(x, t) - g(t)^2\,\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\big]\,dt + g(t)\,d\bar w$$

where $$p_t$$ is the density at time $$t$$ and $$\bar w$$ is a Brownian motion running backwards. Everything in it is known except one term: **the score of the noisy data**, exactly what the networks in parts 4 and 5 learn.

The arrows are the reverse drift at time $$t$$ on a grid, computed with the part 4 network as the score. Scroll from $$t = 1$$ towards 0. At first it gently pulls everything inwards; near $$t = 0$$ it points sharply at the arms of the spiral.

</div>

<div class="tf-step" data-step="reverse" data-title="Sampling with the reverse SDE." markdown="1">
**To sample, solve the reverse SDE** from $$t = 1$$, starting from $$\mathcal{N}(0, I)$$, down to $$t \approx 0$$. With Euler–Maruyama and 200 steps:

$$x \leftarrow x - \big[f(x,t) - g(t)^2 s_\theta(x, t)\big]\Delta t + g(t)\sqrt{\Delta t}\; z$$

with $$s_\theta(x, t) = -\varepsilon_\theta(x, t)/\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha(t)}$$ from the part 4 network. Scroll to run it.

This is not DDPM's Algorithm 2, but it is very close: Algorithm 2 is another discretisation of the same reverse SDE (Song et al. call it ancestral sampling). On 2,000 samples, both land at the same distance from the spiral as real data does.

</div>

<div class="tf-step" data-step="pc" data-title="Predictor–corrector sampling." markdown="1">
**The SDE view suggests a new sampler.** Each solver step (the **predictor**) makes a small error. But at every time $$t$$ we know the score of the distribution we should be in, so we can run a few steps of Langevin dynamics (part 5) at that $$t$$ to pull the cloud back towards it: a **corrector**.

Song et al. set the corrector's step size from a target "signal-to-noise ratio" $$r$$, so that the score's pull and the noise are in a fixed proportion: $$\epsilon = 2\alpha\,(r\,\lVert z\rVert / \lVert s_\theta\rVert)^2$$. With $$r = 0.16$$, as in the paper, the figure compares 20 predictor steps with and without one corrector step each.

On this model the corrector makes little difference (on 2,000 samples at 20 steps: 0.046 with it, 0.044 without), and it doubles the network calls. Song et al. found the same for VP SDEs and dropped the corrector there; it paid off for VE SDEs, which is where their record results came from.

</div>

<div class="tf-step" data-step="steps" data-title="How many steps?" markdown="1">
**The real gain from the SDE view is freedom over the step count.** The network was trained on 200 steps, but the SDE is continuous, so any discretisation works. The chart shows the distance to the spiral after 10 to 200 Euler–Maruyama steps, with and without the corrector. Scroll to see the clouds.

10 steps is too coarse: the cloud is blurred. By 50 steps it's at the level of real data. On 2,000 samples: 0.086 at 10 steps, 0.044 at 20, 0.023 at 50, against 0.025 for 2,000 real points.

Can we go lower? Part 7 removes the noise from the reverse SDE entirely, and gets much better results at 10 and 20 steps.

## The whole thing in a few lines

A diffusion model is defined by a forward SDE with no learned parts,

$$dx = f(x, t)\,dt + g(t)\,dw, \qquad t \in [0, 1]$$

chosen so that it turns data into a known distribution by $$t = 1$$. A network $$s_\theta(x, t)$$ is trained to match the score $$\nabla_x \log p_t(x)$$ at every $$t$$, with denoising score matching:

$$\mathbb{E}_{t}\, \lambda(t)\; \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\, x_t}\Big\lVert s_\theta(x_t, t) - \nabla_{x_t} \log p_{0t}(x_t \mid x_0) \Big\rVert^2$$

where $$p_{0t}$$ is the forward process's Gaussian transition (known in closed form for these SDEs). Samples come from solving the reverse-time SDE, $$dx = [f - g^2 s_\theta]\,dt + g\,d\bar w$$, with any SDE solver.

{: .tf-table}
| | VE SDE | VP SDE | sub-VP SDE |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $$f(x, t)$$ | 0 | $$-\tfrac12\beta(t)\,x$$ | $$-\tfrac12\beta(t)\,x$$ |
| $$g(t)^2$$ | $$d[\sigma^2(t)]/dt$$ | $$\beta(t)$$ | $$\beta(t)\big(1 - e^{-2\int_0^t \beta}\big)$$ |
| discrete version | Song and Ermon (2019) | DDPM (2020) | new in the paper |
| at $$t = 1$$ | $$\mathcal{N}(x_0, \sigma_\text{max}^2 I)$$, huge | $$\approx \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$ | $$\approx \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$ |

With bigger networks (NCSN++ and DDPM++) trained on the continuous objective, the paper reached an FID of 2.20 and an Inception Score of 9.89 on unconditional CIFAR-10, a record at the time, and the first 1024 × 1024 samples from a score-based model (CelebA-HQ). The sub-VP SDE gave a likelihood of 2.99 bits per dimension, computed with the probability flow ODE of part 7.

## Going deeper

### Why the reverse needs the score

Here is the one-dimensional intuition for Anderson's result. Run the forward process for a short time $$dt$$ from a density $$p_t$$. Points spread out by $$g\sqrt{dt}$$ in random directions. Now look backwards: a point at $$x$$ at time $$t + dt$$ more likely came from a direction where $$p_t$$ was higher, simply because more points started there. That bias is proportional to $$\nabla \log p_t$$, and to how far points spread, $$g^2$$. It's the same Bayes'-rule argument as [part 1's small-step derivation]({% post_url 2026-10-29-dm-1-the-idea %}#small-steps), which gave a reverse step centred at $$x_t/\sqrt{\alpha_t} + (\beta_t/\alpha_t)\nabla\log q$$: in the limit, exactly the reverse-time SDE's drift for the VP SDE.

### The densities follow the Fokker–Planck equation

As the cloud evolves under $$dx = f\,dt + g\,dw$$, its density obeys a partial differential equation,

$$\frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t} = -\nabla\cdot\big(f\,p_t\big) + \frac{g^2}{2}\,\nabla^2 p_t$$

drift moves probability around, and diffusion smooths it out. Anderson's reverse SDE and the probability flow ODE of part 7 are two other processes whose densities obey this same equation, run backwards. That's the precise sense in which they "undo" the forward process: not path by path, but distribution by distribution.

### The forward transitions in closed form

Training needs $$x_t$$ given $$x_0$$ in one jump, as in part 2. For these linear SDEs the transition is Gaussian. For the VP SDE,

$$p_{0t}(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha(t)}\,x_0,\; (1 - \bar\alpha(t))\,I\big), \qquad \bar\alpha(t) = e^{-\int_0^t \beta(s)\,ds} = e^{-0.1t - 9.95t^2}$$

the continuous version of part 2's formula. At $$t = 1$$ it gives $$\bar\alpha = 4.3 \times 10^{-5}$$, against $$3.0 \times 10^{-5}$$ for our 200-step chain and $$4.0 \times 10^{-5}$$ for DDPM's 1,000 steps: the discrete chains converge to the SDE as the steps shrink.

### Why the corrector matters more for VE

Our toy and the paper agree that a corrector adds little to a VP sampler. One reason: a VP predictor step already contracts the cloud towards the right scale ($$f = -\tfrac12\beta x$$), and DDPM-style steps already have Langevin's form. The VE SDE has no drift, so all the structure has to come from the score term, and errors in the predictor are not damped by anything. In the paper's experiments, one corrector step per predictor step was what lifted VE models to the best results, and a VE model with $$r = 0.16$$ was their default.

### Controllable generation

One more consequence of the SDE view, which part 9 builds on: to sample from $$p(x \mid y)$$ for some condition $$y$$ (a class, or the known pixels of an image with a hole), replace the score in the reverse SDE by the conditional score,

$$\nabla_x \log p_t(x \mid y) = \nabla_x \log p_t(x) + \nabla_x \log p_t(y \mid x)$$

The first term is the unconditional network. The second can come from a separate classifier trained on noisy inputs, or, for inpainting and colourisation, from the structure of the problem itself. Song et al. did class-conditional generation, inpainting and colourisation this way with a single unconditional model.
