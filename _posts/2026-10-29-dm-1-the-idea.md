Put a drop of ink in a glass of water. Within a minute it has spread into a cloud, and after an hour the glass is evenly tinted. You have never seen the reverse: the tint gathering itself back into a drop. Physics allows it, but it is so unlikely that it never happens.

**Diffusion models** make the reverse happen on purpose. They take some data (photos, sounds, the positions of atoms), destroy it slowly by adding a little noise many times over until nothing is left but static, and then train a neural network to undo one small step of that destruction. Run the network over and over, starting from fresh static, and out comes new data that was never in the training set.

That idea was published in 2015 by Jascha Sohl-Dickstein, Eric Weiss, Niru Maheswaranathan and Surya Ganguli, in [_Deep unsupervised learning using nonequilibrium thermodynamics_](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03585). The image generators people use today (Stable Diffusion, Imagen, DALL·E 2 and 3, Sora) are all descendants of it. This series rebuilds the line from that paper to today's methods, one paper at a time.

This first part is the idea itself, with no more maths than a weighted sum. Everything else in the series is about doing it faster and better.

## The toy data

Image models work on millions of numbers per example, which is impossible to watch. So every figure in this series works on points in a plane, where you can see every single one.

The data in this part is the **Swiss roll**, the same toy the 2015 paper used for its first figure: 800 points scattered along a spiral. Each point is just a pair of numbers $$(x, y)$$. The spiral is scaled so the cloud is centred on the origin with a spread of about 1 in each direction, the same size as the noise it will be turned into.

The network that undoes the noise is small: about 5,700 weights. It was trained on this spiral ahead of time, and it runs in your browser as you scroll. [Part 4]({% post_url 2026-10-29-dm-4-ddpm %}) trains one from scratch, live.

## The whole thing in a few lines

Pick a number of steps $$T$$ and a schedule $$\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T$$ of small noise levels.

1. **Forward process (fixed, nothing to learn).** Turn data $$x_0$$ into noise one step at a time: $$x_t = \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon$$. After $$T$$ steps, $$x_T$$ is practically a standard Gaussian, whatever $$x_0$$ was.
2. **Reverse process (learned).** Train a network to predict, for each noisy $$x_t$$ and step $$t$$, the Gaussian that $$x_{t-1}$$ came from.
3. **Sampling.** Draw $$x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$ and apply the learned reverse step $$T$$ times.

Our toy uses $$T = 200$$ steps. The 2015 paper used 40 steps for its Swiss roll (and a network with 16 hidden units) and 1,000 for images. Image models in the style of part 4 are still trained with 1,000 steps and, as later parts show, can be sampled in far fewer.

## Where diffusion fits

The 2015 paper set out to solve a trade-off that every generative model faces. Simple models, like a single Gaussian, are **tractable**: you can sample from them and compute how likely a point is. Flexible models, like an arbitrary energy function, can fit anything but are usually **intractable**: computing a probability needs a normalising constant nobody can calculate, and sampling needs slow Markov chain Monte Carlo.

Diffusion gets flexibility from a long chain of tractable pieces. Each reverse step is a plain Gaussian, so sampling is exact and fast per step, and the probability of a point can be bounded. The chain as a whole can describe a very complicated distribution.

{: .tf-table}
| family | how it makes a sample | likelihood | what makes it hard |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| VAE | decode one random code | lower bound | blurry samples |
| GAN | one pass of a generator | none | unstable adversarial training |
| normalising flow | one pass of an invertible network | exact | the network must be invertible |
| autoregressive | one dimension at a time | exact | slow: one step per pixel or token |
| diffusion | many small denoising steps | lower bound (exact via an ODE, [part 7]({% post_url 2026-10-29-dm-7-ode-and-ddim %})) | slow: one network call per step |

In 2015 the samples were not competitive with GANs, which had appeared a year earlier. It took five more years, and the paper in part 4, for diffusion models to match them. The ideas were all here already.

## Going deeper

The steps above show _what_ happens. This section is about _why_. Each question stands on its own.

### Why the spread stays near 1 {#variance-preserving}

Suppose $$x_{t-1}$$ has variance $$v$$ along some direction. One step adds independent noise, so the variances add:

$$\operatorname{Var}(x_t) = (1 - \beta_t)\,v + \beta_t$$

If $$v = 1$$, this is $$1 - \beta_t + \beta_t = 1$$. Data with spread 1 keeps spread 1 forever. Data with any other spread is pulled towards 1: the gap $$v - 1$$ shrinks by a factor of $$1 - \beta_t$$ at every step. That's why this process is called **variance preserving**, and why it pays to scale the data to spread 1 to begin with.

Without the $$\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$$ shrink, the noise would keep piling up and the spread would grow without bound. That alternative, **variance exploding**, is the version used by the score-based models of [part 5]({% post_url 2026-10-29-dm-5-scores-and-langevin %}); [part 6]({% post_url 2026-10-29-dm-6-sdes %}) shows the two are the same idea.

### How the exact answers are computed {#exact}

Our Swiss roll is not really 800 loose points. It is defined as a mixture of 160 small round Gaussian blobs strung along the spiral, and the 800 points are drawn from that mixture. That choice makes everything exact.

A Gaussian blob with centre $$\mu$$ and variance $$s^2$$, pushed through $$t$$ steps of the forward process, is still a Gaussian blob: centre $$\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mu$$ and variance $$\bar\alpha_t s^2 + 1 - \bar\alpha_t$$, where $$\bar\alpha_t = (1-\beta_1)(1-\beta_2)\cdots(1-\beta_t)$$. Part 2 derives this. So the noisy data at every step is a known mixture of 160 Gaussians.

The reverse question, "where was this point one step ago?", is Bayes' rule:

$$q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) \;\propto\; q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) \; q(x_{t-1})$$

The first factor is Gaussian in $$x_{t-1}$$, and the second is a mixture of Gaussians. A Gaussian times a Gaussian is a Gaussian, so the answer is again a mixture of 160 Gaussians, with weights and centres in closed form. That's what the right-hand panel shades.

The network never sees any of this. It only sees samples. The exact answers are there to check it.

### Why a small step backwards is Gaussian {#small-steps}

Take Bayes' rule from the last section, and write the first factor as a function of $$x_{t-1}$$. Since $$x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon$$ with $$\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$$, it is a Gaussian bump centred at $$x_t / \sqrt{\alpha_t}$$ with variance $$\beta_t / \alpha_t$$:

$$q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) \;\propto\; \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\alpha_t}{2\beta_t}\,\big\lVert x_{t-1} - x_t/\sqrt{\alpha_t} \big\rVert^2\Big)$$

When $$\beta_t$$ is small, this bump is very narrow. Over such a short distance, the other factor, the density $$q(x_{t-1})$$, hardly changes, so we can replace its logarithm by a straight line (a first-order Taylor expansion around $$\bar{x} = x_t/\sqrt{\alpha_t}$$):

$$\log q(x_{t-1}) \;\approx\; \log q(\bar{x}) + g \cdot (x_{t-1} - \bar{x}), \qquad g = \nabla \log q(\bar{x})$$

A Gaussian times the exponential of a straight line is a Gaussian with the same width, shifted. Completing the square gives

$$q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) \;\approx\; \mathcal{N}\!\Big(\frac{x_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} + \frac{\beta_t}{\alpha_t}\,\nabla \log q\big(x_t/\sqrt{\alpha_t}\big),\;\; \frac{\beta_t}{\alpha_t}\, I\Big)$$

The error in that straight-line approximation shrinks with the width of the bump, so the smaller the step, the closer to Gaussian the reverse step is. In the figure, step 40 has $$\beta_{40} = 0.020$$, so the predicted width is $$\sqrt{0.020/0.980} = 0.143$$; the exact answer has width 0.141, and its distance from a Gaussian is under 0.0001 nats. A jump of 10 steps is still close (0.003 nats); a jump of 30 is not (0.38).

Two things in that formula come back later. The width is set by $$\beta_t$$ alone, which is why part 1 could simply fix it. And the shift is $$\nabla \log q$$, the gradient of the log density, which points uphill towards where the data is. That gradient is called the **score**, and part 5 shows that a diffusion model is, underneath, a network that learns it.

The 2015 paper states this result for continuous diffusions in general, citing the Kolmogorov forward and backward equations as treated by William Feller (1949): for small steps, the reverse of a diffusion has the same functional form as the forward one.

### Measuring how close a cloud is to the data {#measuring}

Every "distance" in the strips is a **sliced Wasserstein distance** from a cloud of points to the exact data distribution, which we know because the toy data is a mixture of Gaussians:

1. Pick a direction, and project every point of the cloud onto it, which turns the cloud into a list of numbers. Along the same direction, the data distribution becomes a mixture of 1D Gaussians, whose cumulative distribution $$F$$ is known.
2. Compare the cloud's cumulative distribution $$F_n$$ (the fraction of its points below each value) with $$F$$, and integrate the gap, $$\int \lvert F_n(x) - F(x) \rvert\, dx$$. In one dimension that is exactly the earth mover's distance: how far you would have to move the cloud's points, on average, to turn them into the data distribution.
3. Repeat for 32 evenly spaced directions and average.

A finite sample is never at distance 0, because it is random. 800 points drawn from the real spiral sit about 0.04 from it (between about 0.03 and 0.05 from one draw to the next). That's the floor: a model can't do better than real data. Pure noise sits about 0.15 from the spiral.

The spread from draw to draw matters. A difference of 0.01 between two 800-point clouds can be luck, so when this series compares two models, the prose quotes measurements made on 4,000 samples, where the floor drops to about 0.02.

### What the "nats" number means

The strip in steps 5 and 6 reports the **Kullback–Leibler divergence** from the true reverse step to the single Gaussian that best matches it (same mean, same covariance):

$$\mathrm{KL}(q \,\Vert\, g) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q}\big[\log q(x) - \log g(x)\big]$$

It is 0 only when the two distributions are identical, and it grows as they differ. It's measured in **nats** (natural-log units; divide by $$\ln 2 \approx 0.69$$ for bits). Here it's estimated by drawing 1,500 points from the true answer and averaging the log ratio. KL divergences are the currency of part 3, where the whole training objective turns out to be a sum of them.

### What the network is

The network in this part is a plain multilayer perceptron:

- **Input**: the point $$(x, y)$$ and 16 numbers that encode the step $$t$$: the sine and cosine of $$t/T$$ at 8 frequencies, the same trick the Transformer uses to encode word positions. One network handles all 200 steps.
- **Three hidden layers** of 48 units with the SiLU activation, $$z / (1 + e^{-z})$$.
- **Output**: two numbers. In this series the network predicts the _noise_ in $$x_t$$, and the centre of the reverse step is computed from that. Part 4 explains why that is the best thing to predict.

That's 5,714 weights in total, trained for 30,000 steps of Adam on batches of 256 noisy points. The training loss is part 4's subject; the script that produced these weights is in the repository, so you can rebuild them yourself.

### Where the physics comes in

The paper's title promises nonequilibrium thermodynamics, and the debt is real, even though you don't need any physics to use the result.

In statistical physics, a system pushed slowly from one state to another passes through a sequence of near-equilibrium states, and a slow, smooth process can be reversed. The paper borrows two tools built on that picture. **Annealed importance sampling** (Neal, 2001) estimates hard probabilities by walking a chain of easier distributions. The **Jarzynski equality** (1997) relates the work done along random forward paths to the free energy difference between the ends.

The paper's model probability is written in exactly that form: the probability of a data point is an average, over forward noising paths from that point, of how much more likely the path is under the learned reverse chain than under the forward chain. When the forward and reverse chains match perfectly, a single path gives the exact answer. Part 3 turns that average into the training objective.
