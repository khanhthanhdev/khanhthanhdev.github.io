    BSP on the NexArm

    B-spline Policy, Diffusion Policy with B-spline actions

    Inference · 7 steps

    1. 01

      One control cycle

      B-spline Policy (BSP) keeps a standard Diffusion Policy and changes what it generates. Instead of a list of future joint targets, it denoises the parameters of a curve: 16 knots and their control points, which define one cubic B-spline segment. The controller samples that curve at 100 Hz. It can also move along it n times faster, and when the next segment arrives it is joined at the point that matches where the arm actually is. This page follows the “Diff.+BSP” variant, the one implemented in the released code and in our bspline-policy fork; it has not been trained on the NexArm yet.

      [\hat U ;\ \hat C] \sim \pi_\theta\big(\cdot \mid o_{t-1},\ o_t\big),\qquad [\hat U ;\ \hat C] \in \mathbb{R}^{16 \times (1 + D)}
      a(u) = \sum_i N_{i,3}(u)\, c_i

      o is one observation (camera frames and joint state); the policy sees the last two. Column 0 holds the 16 knots Û, the other D columns the control points Ĉ (D = 6 on the NexArm). Ni,3 are the cubic B-spline basis functions those knots define.

      In more detail

      Why this matters for speed: an action chunk is a list of targets at the demonstration rate. Running it faster means sending the same targets closer together in time, so each command is a bigger jump from the last, and the step between one chunk and the next becomes harder to track. A curve can be sampled as finely as the controller wants at any speed, and the alignment step removes the jump between segments.

      In the paper’s Speed Stacking figure, plain Diffusion Policy takes 20.5 s and Diffusion Policy with BSP at 4× takes 9.6 s, with visibly smoother gripper paths. The faster run also succeeds less often (7/20 against 14/20 in Table 1), a trade-off the Results tab returns to.

      2 observations → segment 16 × (1 knot + D)
    2. 02

      Observe

      The policy reads the last two observations, each with every camera frame and the joint state, as Diffusion Policy does. The paper uses absolute joint positions as actions on its real arms, which matches the NexArm’s joint-target interface. Two clocks run at once. The policy produces a new segment about 10 times a second, and the controller sends a command 100 times a second by sampling the current segment.

      f_{\text{policy}} = 10\ \text{Hz},\qquad f_{\text{control}} = 100\ \text{Hz}

      The next segment is inferred asynchronously while the arm is still running the current one. The paper samples at 100 Hz; the released rollout script defaults to 200 Hz.

      In more detail

      The two clocks never wait for each other. The paper’s Algorithm 2 launches the next inference in the background as soon as the unexecuted part of the current segment falls below an inference budget, while the controller keeps sampling the current curve. As long as inference returns before the segment runs out, the arm never stops to wait for the network.

      The images are small, 84 × 84, with random 76 × 76 crops during training and a centre crop at inference: the standard Diffusion Policy augmentation. The paper uses a single third-person camera for its pick-and-place tasks and two wrist cameras plus a fixed camera for bimanual Speed Stacking.

      n_obs_steps 2 · images 84×84, random 76×76 crops · state
    3. 03

      Diffusion Policy

      The network is Diffusion Policy’s: a ResNet18 encoder with GroupNorm turns the images into a feature vector, which together with the joint state conditions a 1-D temporal UNet through FiLM. Starting from pure noise, the UNet removes noise from a 16-row matrix in 16 DDIM steps. The only change BSP makes is one extra channel: column 0 of every row is a knot, the other columns are a control point.

      x^{K} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),\qquad x^{k-1} = \operatorname{DDIM}\big(x^{k},\ \epsilon_\theta(x^{k}, k, o)\big)
      x^{0} = [\hat U ;\ \hat C] \in \mathbb{R}^{16 \times (1 + D)}

      εθ is the UNet (down dims 256, 512, 1024; kernel 5) predicting the added noise; K = 16 inference steps of a 100-step squared-cosine schedule.

      In more detail

      Diffusion Policy generates its output by starting from Gaussian noise and repeatedly removing the noise the network predicts. Unlike a regression head, which can only output one answer per observation, it can represent several distinct ways of doing a task and sample one of them. That makes it robust to inconsistent demonstrations.

      The 1-D UNet convolves along the 16 rows, so neighbouring knots and control points are generated together and stay consistent. FiLM conditioning scales and shifts the UNet’s feature channels by amounts computed from the observation features, which is how what the cameras see steers the denoising. Using 16 DDIM steps instead of the 100 the schedule was trained with is a speed trade: DDIM can skip steps deterministically, so one inference costs 16 UNet passes.

      obs features → UNet × 16 steps → 16 × (1 + D)
    4. 04

      Knots and control points

      The knots set where in time each piece of the curve begins and ends, and they do not have to be evenly spaced. The control points set where the curve goes: it follows them without passing through them. A segment is a chunk of 10 knots plus 3 boundary-support knots on each side, 16 rows in all. A cubic spline over 16 knots has 12 control points, so the decoder uses the first 12 rows’ control points and ignores the last 4. Nothing forces the generated knots to be ordered, so any knot that would go backwards is pushed just past its neighbour.

      u_i \leftarrow \max(u_i,\ u_{i-1} + \delta)
      a(u) = \sum_{i=0}^{11} N_{i,3}(u)\, c_i,\qquad u \in [\,u_3,\ u_{12}\,]

      δ is a small positive constant. In practice the paper finds that predicted knots are almost always in order already.

      In more detail

      The fit treats the sample index of the demonstration as time, so a knot value of 7.5 means 7.5 data steps, 0.75 s at 10 Hz. In the training targets the knot column is shifted so that 0 is the current observation; the policy therefore predicts times relative to now.

      When decoding, the curve is valid between the fourth knot u3 and the thirteenth u12 (the second formula). The three knots on each side only support the cubic pieces at the ends. How much real time a segment covers is not fixed: it is whatever span the generated knots describe.

      chunk 10 + 2 × 3 support = 16 knots · 12 control points used
    5. 05

      A weighted sum with local support

      Each control point is weighted by a basis function that is non-zero over only four knot spans. Moving one control point therefore reshapes only that part of the curve, which the lower plot shows. An error in one predicted parameter bends one piece and leaves the rest of the segment unchanged. Cubic pieces also meet with continuous velocity and acceleration, so the commanded motion has no corners.

      N_{i,0}(u) = \begin{cases} 1 & u_i \le u < u_{i+1} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
      N_{i,p}(u) = \frac{u - u_i}{u_{i+p} - u_i} N_{i,p-1}(u) + \frac{u_{i+p+1} - u}{u_{i+p+1} - u_{i+1}} N_{i+1,p-1}(u)
      N_{i,3}(u) \ge 0,\qquad \sum_i N_{i,3}(u) = 1

      The Cox–de Boor recursion, with p = 3. Ni,3 is zero outside [ui, ui+4).

      In more detail

      At any instant only four basis functions are non-zero, so the command at that instant depends on just four control points. The basis functions are also never negative and sum to one everywhere (last formula). The curve therefore always lies inside the convex hull of those four points: it cannot overshoot its control points, which limits how far one bad prediction can send the arm.

      A list of actions has none of these guarantees. Each target is predicted independently of its neighbours, so nothing stops two adjacent targets from disagreeing; with a spline, smoothness is built into the representation.

      a(u) ∈ ℝ⁶ for any u in the segment
    6. 06

      Sample at 100 Hz, run n× faster

      The curve is defined for every u, not only at the recorded steps. The controller can therefore sample it as densely as it likes, and it can speed the motion up by scaling time. At 2× the arm follows the same path in half the time with half as many samples. The policy is not retrained for each speed. The limit is the hardware: at 4× the paper’s low-cost arms could no longer track the bimanual Speed Stacking task.

      a_{\text{exec}}(t) = a(n\,t)
      \dot a_{\text{exec}}(t) = n\, \dot a(n t),\qquad \ddot a_{\text{exec}}(t) = n^{2}\, \ddot a(n t)

      n is the speedup factor and t is real time since the segment started.

      In more detail

      In Algorithm 2 the curve parameter is u = n · (now − t0). Speeding up changes nothing in the network; it only changes how fast the controller walks along u. By the chain rule, velocities scale by n and accelerations by n² (second formula). That is why 4× is far harder on the motors than 2×: four times the speed needs sixteen times the acceleration.

      The paper’s baselines speed up the plain chunk by raising the command rate by n. The arm then visits the same targets, but nothing is filled in between them, and the jump between chunks grows with the speed.

      1× · 100 samples per second of curve · n× · 100 / n
    7. 07

      Join the next segment smoothly

      By the time a new segment arrives, the arm has moved on, and noise and tracking error mean the new curve does not start exactly where the old one is. Switching at a fixed time would make the command jump. BSP instead searches the start of the new segment for the point closest to the last executed action, and continues from there. Without this step, Speed Stacking success at 4× falls from 35 % to 15 % in the paper’s ablation, which uses the Diffusion backbone.

      t^{\star} = \arg\min_{0 \le t \le \lambda T_{\text{inf}}} \operatorname{MSE}\big(S_{\text{new}}(t),\ a_{\text{last}}\big)
      t_0 \leftarrow \text{now} - t^{\star} / n,\qquad u = n\,(\text{now} - t_0)

      Tinf is the measured inference latency and λ > 1 widens the window. On the real arms the window is half the segment: λTinf = umax(S) / 2.

      In more detail

      The whole loop of Algorithm 2 runs at the control rate:

      • sample the current segment at u = n · (now − t0) and send the command;
      • when less than an inference budget of the segment is left, start a new inference in the background;
      • when it returns, search the new segment for t⋆, swap segments, and reset the clock so that u continues from t⋆ (second formula).

      The search is a one-dimensional minimization over a short window, cheap compared with the network. The paper applies it on the real arms only: with delta end-effector actions in simulation, “closest to the last action” has no clear meaning. The fork’s rollout script has options for it: --disable-time-align, --time-align-larger-t, --time-align-error-threshold (default 0.1) and --consider-gripper-during-align.

      no jump at segment boundaries

    Training · 7 steps

    1. 01

      One training step

      Training is ordinary Diffusion Policy training with different targets. Every demonstration is first converted into a B-spline with an adaptive fit. At each observation the target is the next 16 knots and their control points, and the UNet learns to denoise them. The figures use a toy single-joint trajectory; the fitting, the windows and the basis functions are computed from it, not drawn by hand.

      \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{k,\ \epsilon}\ \big\lVert \epsilon_\theta\big(x^{k}, k, o\big) - \epsilon \big\rVert^2,\qquad x^{0} = [U ; C]

      xk is the target matrix with noise of level k added; ε is that noise.

      In more detail

      Because only the targets change, everything else in a Diffusion Policy setup carries over: the encoder, the noise schedule, the optimizer and the EMA. The conversion runs once, before training, and is cached on disk next to the dataset (.bspline_sampler_*.npz). The cache is keyed on the fit settings, so changing ε or the chunk size triggers a new fit.

    2. 02

      Demonstrations

      A demonstration is a list of joint targets at a fixed rate. The paper records 200 to 300 teleoperated demonstrations per real task. A pick-and-place has fast, coarse parts (reach, carry, retreat) and slow, precise parts (grasp, place). A fixed-rate chunk spends the same resolution on both.

      \{(t_i,\ a_i)\}_{i=0}^{N-1},\qquad t_i = i,\qquad a_i \in \mathbb{R}^{D}

      In more detail

      The fit uses the sample index as time, so the curve’s parameter is measured in data steps. The fork’s real-robot config and rollout script use 10 Hz data (--data-freq 10), so one unit of u is 0.1 s. Each episode is fitted as a whole, all action dimensions at once with a single shared knot vector.

      toy · one joint · 50 Hz · 8 s · 401 samples
    3. 03

      Adaptive B-spline fit

      Algorithm 1 of the paper starts with no interior knots and fits a cubic B-spline by least squares. It checks the largest error and, if it is above the tolerance, inserts a knot where the residual is largest (the FITPACK criterion), then repeats. The code does this with SciPy’s FITPACK knot generator and make_lsq_spline. Knots end up where the motion bends and are sparse where it is simple.

      E = \max_i \lVert a(t_i) - a_i \rVert \le \varepsilon
      C^{\star} = \arg\min_{C} \sum_i \big\lVert a(t_i;\ U, C) - a_i \big\rVert^2

      The paper and the fork’s real-robot config use ε = 0.002 (max_error) to keep precise motions. The toy fit uses ε = 0.004 rad and needs 41 knots for 401 samples.

      In more detail

      For a fixed knot vector, the control points are the ordinary least-squares solution (second formula): the curve that passes as close as possible to every sample. Inserting a knot adds freedom exactly where the curve is worst. The loop stops at the first knot vector whose largest error is within ε, taken over all samples and all joints. If none is, the code keeps the last one it tried and prints a warning.

      With ε = 0.002 in joint units (about 0.11° if the joints are in radians), the fit is close to lossless for position targets, yet a smooth reach needs only a handful of knots.

    4. 04

      Fixed-size targets

      Observations do not line up with knots, so at each observation step the target is the nearest future window of 16 knots and their control points. Because knot spacing varies, 16 knots can cover a long time where the motion is simple and a short time where it is detailed. Near the end of an episode the last control point is repeated to fill the window.

      x^{0} = \big[\,u_{j:j+16} - t \ ;\ c_{j:j+16}\,\big],\qquad j = \min\{\, j : u_{j+3} \ge t \,\}

      In more detail

      With stride: 1, the fork’s real-robot setting, a window starts at every knot of the fitted curve. Each observation step is given the first window whose first interior knot uj+3 lies at or after it (the formula), so the target always begins at or just after “now”. Consecutive observations can share one window, each with the knot column shifted by its own time.

      16 × (1 + D) per sample · knot times in data steps, 0 = the current observation
    5. 05

      Denoising loss

      As in Diffusion Policy, a random noise level is picked, that much noise is added to the target matrix, and the UNet, conditioned on the observations, predicts the noise. The loss is the squared error of that prediction. The knot column is learned exactly like the control points. For a fair comparison, the paper’s chunking baselines predict 16 future actions with the same visual encoder and the same number of epochs.

      x^{k} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_k}\, x^{0} + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_k}\, \epsilon
      \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),\qquad k \sim \mathcal{U}\{1, \dots, 100\}
      \mathcal{L} = \big\lVert \epsilon_\theta(x^{k}, k, o_{t-1}, o_t) - \epsilon \big\rVert^2

      AdamW, lr 10-4 with 500 warm-up steps and a cosine schedule, batch 64, EMA of the weights.

      In more detail

      Before noise is added, the target is normalized with dataset statistics, as Diffusion Policy does for its action chunks. The knot column, measured in data steps, and the joint columns therefore end up on comparable scales, and the knot column needs no special treatment in the loss.

      The noise level k is drawn uniformly for every sample, so a single batch trains the UNet on almost-clean and almost-pure-noise inputs alike. The fork’s config trains for 601 epochs.

    6. 06

      16 actions vs 16 knots

      A 16-step chunk at 10 Hz always covers 1.6 s at an even spacing. The 16 knots of a segment are placed by the fit, so detail goes where the trajectory needs it. This is the “adaptive temporal resolution” the paper argues fixed chunks lack.

      T_{\text{chunk}} = 16 \times 0.1\,\text{s} = 1.6\,\text{s}\ \ \text{(always)}
      T_{\text{segment}} = (u_{12} - u_3) \times 0.1\,\text{s}\ \ \text{(set by the knots)}

      In more detail

      On a long, simple reach the 16 knots may span several seconds; around a grasp they may span a fraction of one. The network does not choose a horizon explicitly: the horizon is implied by the knot values it generates. A fixed chunk has to compromise. If it is long, it is coarse at the grasp; if it is short, it re-plans needlessly often during the reach.

    7. 07

      Fit tolerance

      A looser tolerance needs fewer knot–control-point pairs. On Push-T the compression ratio rises from 1.12× at ε = 0.5 to 3.34× at ε = 8, while the score stays between 0.52 and 0.65. Performance is not very sensitive to ε within a sensible range. These are the paper’s numbers (Table 3); Push-T actions are pixel positions up to about 512, which is why ε is so much larger there.

      \text{compression} = \frac{\#\ \text{recorded actions}}{\#\ \text{knot–control-point pairs}}

      In more detail

      ε is measured in the units of the actions, which is why it has to be chosen per task. A larger tolerance means fewer knots, so each 16-knot window covers more time and every prediction reaches further ahead. A smaller tolerance keeps more detail but spends the window on a shorter stretch. The paper’s ablation suggests the policy is not very sensitive to the choice within a sensible range.

    Training details

    A Diffusion Policy that denoises the knots and control points of a cubic B-spline instead of a list of actions; the controller samples the curve at 100 Hz and can run it several times faster.

    • 16 · knots per segment
    • cubic · spline degree
    • 10 Hz · policy rate
    • 100 Hz · control rate
    • 16 × (1 + D) · output per segment
    • 0.002 · fit tolerance ε

    Architecture

    BSP is a change of action representation, not a new network. The paper adds it to two backbones, Diffusion Policy (“Diff.+BSP”) and an ACT-style regression transformer (“Reg.+BSP”). The released code, and our fork of it, implement the Diffusion Policy variant, so this page follows that one.

    The change sits between the network and the motors. A standard Diffusion Policy denoises a 16 × D matrix of future joint targets. BSP denoises a 16 × (1 + D) matrix instead. The extra column holds knot times, and the remaining columns hold control points. A B-spline evaluator turns those parameters into a smooth curve, and the controller samples that curve at 100 Hz, optionally n times faster, while the next segment is inferred in the background.

    Three properties of cubic B-splines do the work:

    • Continuity. Velocity and acceleration are continuous inside a segment, so dense sampling never creates corners.
    • Local support. Each control point affects only four knot spans, so one bad parameter bends only one piece of the curve.
    • Free knot placement. The knot spacing is learned, so a fixed-size output can cover a long, simple motion or a short, detailed one.

    The one part that is not about the curve itself is segment alignment. When a new segment arrives, it is joined at the point closest to where the arm actually is, instead of at a fixed time.

    Training

    Demonstrations are converted once, before training: each trajectory is fitted with a cubic B-spline by FITPACK knot insertion until the largest error is below ε = 0.002, and the result is cut into 16-knot windows. The UNet then learns to denoise those windows, in the same place where Diffusion Policy would denoise an action chunk.

    Nothing else about training changes: same encoder, same noise schedule, same optimizer, same EMA. The paper’s baselines are trained for the same number of epochs on 16-action chunks, so the comparison isolates the action representation. Because the fit is cached on disk and keyed on its settings, trying another tolerance or segment size costs one refit, not a change of code.

    Setting Value
    Fit SciPy FITPACK knots + least squares, max_error 0.002
    Target nearest future 16 knots + control points, last point repeated
    Loss squared error of the predicted noise
    Optimizer AdamW, lr 1e-4, betas (0.95, 0.999), 500 warm-up steps, cosine, EMA
    Baselines 16-action chunks, same encoder, same number of epochs
    Real tasks Cube Picking, Table Cleaning, Speed Stacking; 200–300 demos each

    Not trained on the NexArm yet. The architecture and settings come from the bspline-policy code and the paper. The figures use a toy single-joint trajectory: the fit, the knot windows, the basis functions and the alignment are computed from it, but it is not a NexArm recording.

    Results

    Evaluation status

    Not yet measured

    BSP has not been trained or run on the NexArm yet. The planned evaluation is the same as for ACT and TurboVLA, plus the speedup at which success starts to fall. For reference, the paper reports Diffusion Policy results on 6-DoF ARX5 arms. At 4×, BSP cut Cube Picking from 3.52 s to 2.45 s (20/20 both) and Table Cleaning from 23.31 s to 18.05 s (12/20 vs 11/20). At 1×, it raised Table Cleaning success from 11/20 to 15/20. On bimanual Speed Stacking at 4× it was faster (9.62 s vs 10.49 s) but succeeded less often (7/20 vs 9/20).

    Metric Value How it is measured
    Stacking success rate — Fraction of trials that end with the bowls nested and released, judged from the front camera.
    Time to stack — Seconds from the first action to the gripper opening over the stack, successful trials only.
    Achieved speedup — Time to stack at 1× divided by time to stack at the target speedup (2×, 4×).
    Command smoothness — Peak joint acceleration of the executed commands, BSP vs the plain Diffusion Policy chunk at the same speed.

    Protocol

    1. Fixed bowl start poses drawn from the recorded workspace, the same list for every policy.
    2. Each policy is run at 1×, 2× and 4×; for the chunk baseline the speedup is a faster command rate.
    3. A trial ends at success, at a collision, or after 60 seconds.
