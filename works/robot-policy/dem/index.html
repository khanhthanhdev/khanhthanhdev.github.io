    DEM on the NexArm

    DEM, the Decoupled Embodiment Model

    Inference · 9 steps

    1. 01

      One control cycle

      DEM reads the cameras, the joint state and the task instruction, and generates a chunk of 16 actions instead of regressing one. A vision encoder, a language encoder and an action head are three separate networks: the head reads the other two only through cross-attention. It starts from Gaussian noise and removes it in a single step, so sampling costs one forward pass. The NexArm runs the first 8 actions, 0.4 seconds, then looks again.

      \hat{A}_t = \varepsilon - u_\theta\big(\varepsilon,\ r{=}0,\ t{=}1 \mid C(I_t^{1..V},\ \ell,\ s_t)\big),\quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)

      C is the context built from V camera views, the instruction ℓ and the six-number joint state; uθ is the average velocity the head predicts over the whole interval from noise (t = 1) to data (r = 0). The result is 16 rows of five joint targets and a gripper target, z-scored.

      In more detail

      A regressor such as ACT or TurboVLA predicts one chunk per observation. When the demonstrations show two ways to do the same thing, an L1 or L2 loss pulls it towards the average of the two, which may be neither. A generative head learns the whole distribution of chunks instead and samples one of them.

      Diffusion and flow-matching policies pay for that with many network passes per chunk. DEM uses MeanFlow: the head learns the average velocity over an interval rather than the instantaneous one, so the whole path from noise to an action chunk is a single step. On the rollout computer’s RTX 5050 one 16-action chunk with three cameras takes 22 ms and 1.8 GB.

      “Decoupled” refers to the three towers: the vision and language encoders know nothing about the robot, and only the head, which reads them through cross-attention, is specific to this arm.

      images V × 3×256×256 · text 32 tokens · state 6 → chunk 16 × 6
    2. 02

      Observe

      Every camera frame arrives as a float image in [0, 1] and is resized to 256 × 256 with antialiasing, in the order front, wrist, top. The joint state is z-scored with the dataset’s mean and standard deviation. ImageNet normalization happens later, inside the vision encoder.

      \tilde{s} = (s - \mu_s) \oslash \sigma_s
      \tilde{I} = \operatorname{resize}_{256}(I),\quad I \in [0, 1]^{3 \times 480 \times 640}

      μs, σs come from the training set and are saved with the checkpoint. The camera order is --policy.camera_keys, or the dataset’s order when it is empty.

      In more detail

      The paper scales state and action in two stages: to [-1, 1] with the 1st and 99th percentiles, then z-scores the result. Both stages are affine per dimension, so together they are one affine map, and LeRobot’s plain z-score on the raw values gives exactly the same numbers. The plugin therefore uses LeRobot’s normalizer and leaves the model’s own normalizers as the identity.

      On the real NexArm data the per-joint standard deviations are 113 to 407 servo ticks, so no joint’s z-score is close to dividing by zero.

      V = 3 views · 640×480 → 256×256 · state 6 z-scored
    3. 03

      DINOv3 ConvNeXt-B

      Each view goes through DINOv3 ConvNeXt-B on its own, under bf16 autocast. DEM takes the stage-3 feature map, at stride 16: a 256-pixel image becomes a 16 × 16 grid of 512-wide features, flattened row by row into 256 tokens. The encoder is fine-tuned with the head, at half the head’s learning rate.

      F = \operatorname{ConvNeXt}_{\le 3}(\tilde I) \in \mathbb{R}^{512 \times 16 \times 16}
      V = \operatorname{flatten}(F)^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times 512}
      N_v = V_{\text{cams}} \cdot (256 / 16)^2 = 256\,V_{\text{cams}}

      ConvNeXt has four stages with strides 4, 8, 16 and 32. The final stage would give 8 × 8 tokens of width 1024; stage 3 keeps four times as many, smaller ones.

      In more detail

      Most VLAs use a vision transformer. ConvNeXt is a convolutional network with transformer-style design choices, and the DINOv3 version is trained by the same self-supervised method as DINOv3’s ViTs, so its features carry the same knowledge of where things are. A convolutional backbone keeps a spatial grid at every stage, which is why DEM can stop at stage 3 and keep a finer grid than the final layer gives.

      Stage-3 tokens are a compromise between detail and cost. 256 tokens per view are enough to locate the bowls and the gripper, and the head’s cross-attention over three views stays cheap.

      V × 3×256×256 → V × 512×16×16 → 768 × 512 with 3 views
    4. 04

      Frozen NeoBERT

      The instruction, such as “stack the red, blue and black bowls”, is tokenized and encoded by NeoBERT, a modern BERT encoder that stays frozen. DEM keeps 32 tokens of width 768, special tokens included, with a mask over padding. Each distinct instruction is encoded once and reused.

      L = \operatorname{NeoBERT}(\ell)_{[1:32]} \in \mathbb{R}^{32 \times 768}
      m_j = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{token } j \text{ is real} \\ 0 & \text{token } j \text{ is padding} \end{cases}

      An empty task falls back to --policy.default_task. The mask keeps padding out of every cross-attention that reads the language tokens.

      In more detail

      Freezing the language encoder keeps what it learned from text and costs nothing in memory at training time, since its activations need no gradient. It still ships in the checkpoint (1.6 GB with it), so loading a trained policy needs no Hub download apart from the tokenizer.

      Instruction following was the first thing to fail on the NexArm. With the paper’s head, six stacking orders that only the prompt tells apart gave the same chunk: across the six prompts the chunk changed by 0.3 servo ticks, against 25 between two noise draws. The next steps explain why, and what the plugin changes.

      instruction → 32 × 768 · frozen
    5. 05

      Build the context

      The three kinds of tokens are projected to the head’s width of 768, tagged with a learned embedding for their modality and concatenated. The joint state becomes a single token. With three cameras this context holds 801 tokens: 768 image tokens, 32 language tokens and one state token.

      C = \big[\ V W_v + e_v \ \Vert\ L W_l + e_l \ \Vert\ \tilde s\, W_p + e_p\ \big]
      N_C = 256 \cdot 3 + 32 + 1 = 801

      Wv is 512 → 768, Wl is 768 → 768 and Wp is 6 → 768; ev, el, ep are learned modality vectors. With action history on, 10 more tokens are appended.

      In more detail

      The context is the head’s only view of the observation in the paper’s design. That is a weakness on the NexArm: the instruction is 32 of 801 tokens and the state is 1, and cross-attention can learn to read the image tokens and ignore the rest. A head trained that way stacked the bowls in a fixed order whatever the prompt said.

      With action history (TEMPO-ACT, off by default), the mean normalized action of each of the last 10 seconds joins the context as 10 more tokens with a learned position each. A single frame cannot say which bowl comes next; the gripper’s past closings can.

      768 + 32 + 1 = 801 tokens × 768
    6. 06

      Global conditioning (NexArm)

      The plugin adds a second path for the instruction and the state. Four learned queries pool the language tokens by attention, the state is projected, and an MLP turns both into one vector g. It is added to the time embedding that modulates every head block through adaptive layer norm, so the head cannot ignore it.

      g = \operatorname{MLP}\Big(\operatorname{LN}\big[\ \operatorname{Pool}_{k=1..4}(L W) \ \Vert\ \tilde s\, W_q\ \big]\Big)
      c = \operatorname{emb}_r(r) + \operatorname{emb}_t(t) + g

      The last layer of the MLP starts at zero, so at step 0 the head computes exactly the paper’s head. --policy.global_conditioning=false restores the paper.

      In more detail

      Attention pooling rather than a plain mean: the stacking prompts share most of their words, and a mean is dominated by the shared ones. Learned queries can attend to the colour words that differ.

      With this change the state was used at once: swapping the state changed the chunk by 282 ticks, against 1.2 with the paper’s head. The instruction was still too weak to separate six stacking orders (0.7 ticks), which is why the reported runs train on a single order.

      4 queries × 768 + state 768 → g · 768
    7. 07

      MeanFlow action head

      The noisy chunk enters as 16 action tokens with a learned position each. Eight blocks of width 768 with 12 heads process them. Each block is a DiT block, self-attention and an MLP modulated by c through adaptive layer norm, followed by gated cross-attention into the 801 context tokens. A final layer norm and linear layer give the average velocity u.

      h \leftarrow h + \gamma_1(c) \odot \operatorname{SelfAttn}\big(\operatorname{LN}(h)(1 + \alpha_1(c)) + \beta_1(c)\big)
      h \leftarrow h + \gamma_2(c) \odot \operatorname{MLP}\big(\operatorname{LN}(h)(1 + \alpha_2(c)) + \beta_2(c)\big)
      h \leftarrow h + \lambda \odot \operatorname{CrossAttn}\big(\operatorname{LN}(h),\ C\big)
      u = W_{\text{out}}\,\operatorname{LN}(h) \in \mathbb{R}^{16 \times 6}

      α, β, γ come from one zero-initialized linear layer on c; the per-channel gate λ starts at zero, as does Wout. About 107M parameters (the paper’s new100m size).

      In more detail

      Everything that can start at zero does. A fresh block is a plain transformer block with closed residual gates and no cross-attention, and the output is zero. Training opens each path only as far as it helps, which keeps the large pretrained features stable while the head is still random.

      Attention is written as explicit matrix products rather than a fused kernel. Training differentiates the head with a forward-mode Jacobian-vector product, which the fused attention kernels do not support.

      16 tokens × 768 → 8 × (AdaLN block + cross-attn) → 16 × 6
    8. 08

      One step from noise

      MeanFlow predicts the average velocity over an interval, so the displacement from t to r is a single product. Sampling uses the whole interval at once: draw noise ε, evaluate the head at r = 0 and t = 1, and subtract. Undoing the z-score gives joint and gripper targets.

      z_r = z_t - (t - r)\,u(z_t, r, t)
      \hat A = \varepsilon - u_\theta(\varepsilon,\ 0,\ 1 \mid C),\qquad A = \hat A \odot \sigma_a + \mu_a

      --policy.num_inference_steps > 1 splits [1, 0] into equal parts and applies the same update per part; the paper and the plugin use 1.

      In more detail

      Fresh noise for every chunk means two consecutive chunks can land in different modes of the distribution, and the arm jumps where one hands over to the next. Two rollout options make chunks agree without retraining: --inference-noise fixed draws one noise per episode and reuses it (zero starts from the noise mean), so the chunk depends on the observation alone; --temporal-ensemble 0.01 predicts a chunk every step and runs the weighted mean of every chunk’s prediction for that step, as in the ACT paper. The ensemble needs the 22 ms inference to fit inside one 50 ms control step, which it does.

      ε 16 × 6 → 1 head pass → 16 × 6 actions
    9. 09

      Run 8, then infer again

      The policy queues the first 8 of the 16 actions and returns one per control step at 20 Hz. When the queue is empty it captures new frames and runs the whole network again. In the 3D view, the dots ahead of the gripper are the actions still to run before the next inference.

      \Delta t_{\text{replan}} = 8 \times \tfrac{1}{20}\,\text{s} = 0.4\,\text{s}

      In more detail

      Like TurboVLA, DEM discards the second half of every chunk, the part predicted furthest ahead from an old view. At 22 ms per chunk the network uses about 5 % of the 0.4 s it buys, so re-planning more often is affordable.

      With action history on, every action the policy returns is also remembered and averaged into the history buckets of later chunks. That needs the synchronous rollout engine (lerobot-rollout’s default), which calls the policy once per step; the RTC engine would leave the history empty.

      8 × 50 ms = 0.4 s between observations

    Training · 7 steps

    1. 01

      One training step

      lerobot-train repeats this loop: load a chunk of 16 actions with the observation, mix it with noise at a random time, ask the head for the average velocity, compare it with a MeanFlow target computed from the head itself, add a dispersive term, and update the head and the vision encoder. NeoBERT stays frozen.

      \theta^{*} = \arg\min_\theta\ \mathbb{E}_{(o, x),\ \varepsilon,\ (r, t)}\ \Big[\ w\,\big\lVert u_\theta(z_t, r, t \mid o) - u_{\text{tgt}} \big\rVert^2\ \Big] + 0.25\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{disp}}

      o is the observation, x the recorded chunk (z-scored), ε Gaussian noise and w an adaptive weight. The target utgt is computed with a stop-gradient.

      In more detail

      The real-arm run trains on the 147 training episodes of the real stacking set for 20,000 updates of 32 samples, about 20 passes over the data. Three cameras at batch 32 do not fit in 24 GB, so each update accumulates two micro-batches of 16: 14.7 GB at 0.37 s per micro-batch on an RTX A5000.

      The model and loss defaults are the paper’s. The conditioning and the learning rates are not, for reasons the steps below give.

    2. 02

      Real demonstrations, idle start cut

      164 teleoperated stacking episodes (20 fps, front, wrist and top cameras); the last 10 % are held out for the loss. Each episode is first trimmed to start 3 frames before the leader moves more than 10 ticks, which removes 11.9 % of the frames. Every sample loads the current observation and the next 16 actions.

      \text{action indices} = [\,-H, \dots, -1,\ 0, \dots, 15\,],\quad H = \text{buckets} \times \text{frames per bucket}

      H = 0 by default. With action history, H = 10 × 20 = 200 past actions are loaded too and averaged per second. Steps past the episode end repeat its last action.

      In more detail

      Every real episode began with about 1.6 seconds of the leader held still while recording ran, 13 % of all frames. Over those frames the images and the state barely change, yet the 16-step target goes from “hold” to “hold, then start”, because only the operator knew when to start. A policy trained on them learns to hesitate at the rest pose. The still ends are kept: there the bowls are stacked and holding still is right.

      147 train · 17 held out · 32,016 training frames after trimming
    3. 03

      Mix the chunk with noise

      Two times are drawn from a logit-normal distribution and sorted, t the larger and r the smaller. In half the samples r is set equal to t. The chunk x is interpolated linearly towards Gaussian noise; along that straight line the instantaneous velocity is known in closed form.

      r, t = \operatorname{sort}\big(\sigma(n_1), \sigma(n_2)\big),\quad n_i \sim \mathcal{N}(-0.4,\ 1)
      z_t = (1 - t)\,x + t\,\varepsilon,\qquad v = \varepsilon - x

      ratio_r_eq_t = 0.5 is the fraction with r = t. t = 0 is the data end and t = 1 the noise end.

      In more detail

      When r = t the interval has zero length, the average velocity equals the instantaneous one, and the loss reduces to ordinary flow matching. Those samples anchor the head to the true velocity field; the samples with r < t teach it to jump across an interval, which is what makes one-step sampling possible.

      x, ε, z_t · B × 16 × 6 · r, t · B
    4. 04

      MeanFlow target

      Differentiating the definition of the average velocity gives the MeanFlow identity: the average velocity equals the instantaneous one minus (t − r) times its own rate of change. That rate is one Jacobian-vector product through the head, along the direction the sample moves.

      u(z_t, r, t) = \frac{1}{t - r}\int_r^t v(z_\tau, \tau)\,d\tau
      (u,\ \tfrac{du}{dt}) = \operatorname{jvp}\big(u_\theta,\ (z_t, r, t),\ (v, 0, 1)\big)
      u_{\text{tgt}} = \operatorname{sg}\big(v - (t - r)\,\tfrac{du}{dt}\big)

      sg is the stop-gradient. torch.func.jvp gives u and du/dt in one forward-mode pass; the head, the JVP and the loss run in fp32 even when training autocasts the rest.

      In more detail

      The target depends on the network being trained, but the stop-gradient turns it into a fixed regression target for each step. The forward-mode JVP costs about one extra forward pass, far less than backpropagating through a sampler.

      It is also why attention in the head is written by hand: fused attention kernels do not support forward-mode differentiation.

      u, du/dt · B × 16 × 6
    5. 05

      Adaptive loss and dispersion

      The squared error is weighted per sample by the inverse square root of its own size, so a few large errors cannot dominate the batch. A dispersive term on the head’s middle hidden state pushes the representations of different samples apart.

      \delta = \tfrac{1}{16 \cdot 6}\lVert u_\theta - u_{\text{tgt}} \rVert^2,\qquad w = \operatorname{sg}\big((\delta + 10^{-3})^{-1/2}\big)
      \mathcal{L}_{\text{disp}} = \log \operatorname{mean}_{i,j}\ e^{-\lVert f_i - f_j \rVert^2 / 0.5}
      \mathcal{L} = \frac{\operatorname{mean}(w\,\delta)}{\operatorname{mean}(w)} + 0.25\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{disp}}

      fi is sample i’s hidden state after the fifth of the eight blocks, averaged over the 16 tokens and normalized to unit length.

      In more detail

      The dispersive term is a log-mean-exp, so it is at most zero, and the total loss can go negative. The adaptive weight also divides out large errors. Both hide trouble, so the number to watch is meanflow_mse, the unweighted δ. With the paper’s learning rates it spiked to 12,554 before the rate decayed; with the halved rates and warm-up the real run went from 1.95 to 0.06.

      δ, w · B · L_disp ≤ 0
    6. 06

      Hide history and views

      Two optional regularizers act on the conditioning. History dropout hides the whole action history in 20 % of the samples. View masking hides each listed camera’s tokens from the head’s cross-attention in 30 % of the samples.

      \text{history mask} \leftarrow \text{history mask} \wedge \neg\,b,\quad b \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.2)
      m^{\text{vis}}_{\text{cam}} \leftarrow 0 \ \text{for all 256 tokens},\quad \text{w.p. } 0.3 \text{ per listed camera}

      --policy.history_dropout, --policy.camera_dropout_keys (front and top in the NexArm run) and --policy.camera_dropout_prob. Both need global conditioning.

      In more detail

      Without history dropout the policy could lean on its own past motion and copy it, and it would never see the empty history of an episode’s first second. View masking makes it act when the arm occludes a camera, or when a camera has moved since recording: --hide-cameras front at rollout hides that view on every call. On this data the same masking gave ACT its lowest held-out error, 64.7 against 71.0 ticks.

      history 10 tokens · 256 tokens per hidden camera
    7. 07

      Optimizer step and EMA

      AdamW updates two parameter groups: the head at 5 × 10-5 and the vision encoder at 2.5 × 10-5, half the paper’s rates. The rate warms up over 2,000 updates and then follows a cosine to zero. An exponential moving average of the weights is kept, and the averaged copy is what runs on the arm.

      \eta(u) = \eta_0 \begin{cases} u / 2000 & u < 2000 \\[4pt] \tfrac{1}{2}\big(1 + \cos(\pi p)\big) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
      \bar\theta \leftarrow 0.999\,\bar\theta + 0.001\,\theta

      Betas (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 10-4, gradient clipping at 1.0. p is the fraction of the decay done.

      In more detail

      At the paper’s 10-4 and 5 × 10-5 without warm-up, the MeanFlow error spiked until the learning rate had decayed. Halving both and adding a warm-up made training stable.

      The held-out loss of the real run was lowest at 8,000 updates (16k micro-batches) and rose slightly after that, so late in training the model fits the 147 training episodes more closely than it generalizes. The final EMA weights are on the rollout computer; a checkpoint nearer the minimum is kept on the training server.

    Architecture

    DEM is built from three networks that never see each other’s weights. A vision encoder (DINOv3 ConvNeXt-B) turns each camera view into 256 tokens. A language encoder (NeoBERT, frozen) turns the instruction into 32 tokens. A MeanFlow action head reads both through cross-attention and maps Gaussian noise to a 16-step action chunk in one forward pass.

    The head is where DEM differs from the other policies here. ACT and TurboVLA regress one chunk per observation; DEM samples one from a learned distribution, as a diffusion policy would, but in a single step instead of dozens. That keeps it as fast as a regressor: 22 ms per chunk with three cameras on the rollout computer’s RTX 5050.

    Part Setting in lerobot-nexarm
    Vision DINOv3 ConvNeXt-B, 256 × 256, stage-3 tokens (16 × 16 × 512 per view), trained
    Language NeoBERT, frozen, 32 tokens × 768
    Context 768 image + 32 language + 1 state token = 801, width 768, modality embeddings
    Head 8 × (AdaLN self-attention block + gated cross-attention), d 768, 12 heads, 107M
    Global condition 4 attention-pooled language queries + state → AdaLN (NexArm addition)
    Sampling MeanFlow, 1 step, fresh noise per chunk (fixed and zero optional)
    Chunk / executed 16 / 8 actions at 20 Hz
    Inference 22 ms and 1.8 GB per chunk, three cameras, RTX 5050

    Changes from the paper

    The paper’s trained weights are not public, so every NexArm run starts from the public DINOv3 and NeoBERT checkpoints. A first run with the paper’s recipe on all six stacking orders failed in sim (0/10): the head ignored both the instruction and the state, and its MeanFlow error spiked up to 12,554 until the learning rate had decayed. The plugin changes two things and keeps the rest:

    Change Why
    Global conditioning The instruction and the state also enter every head block through AdaLN, so hundreds of image tokens cannot drown them out. Starts at zero.
    Halved learning rates 5e-5 head and 2.5e-5 vision with 2,000 warm-up updates, instead of 1e-4 and 5e-5 without warm-up, which made the MeanFlow error unstable.
    Action history Optional (TEMPO-ACT): the mean action of each of the last 10 s, as context tokens and through AdaLN, so the head knows the phase of the task.
    View masking Optional: each listed camera hidden in 30 % of training samples, so the policy still acts when the arm blocks a view.
    Smoother rollouts Optional at inference: one fixed noise per episode, or ACT-style temporal ensembling over a chunk predicted every step.

On a checkpoint, also check that the chunk changes when the instruction or the state does: with the paper’s head it changed by 0.3 ticks across six prompts, against 25 between two noise draws.

    Results

    Evaluation status

    Measured

    Measured in simulation only. On the MuJoCo stack-bowls task with one fixed order, DEM stacked all three bowls in 5 of 20 held-out layouts; grasping, carrying and nesting work, and every failure picked the top bowl first. The real-arm runs (real data only, and real data with history, view masking and augmentation) are trained but not yet rolled out.

    Metric Value How it is measured
    Sim success, single order 25 % (5/20) MuJoCo, order red, blue, black, 20 held-out layouts, EMA weights after 50k steps on 500 scripted episodes; 95 % CI 11–47 %.
    Picked the top bowl first 90 % Same 20 episodes. The first pick follows the black bowl across all three slots, so it is not a position shortcut.
    Sim success, six orders 0/10 The paper’s recipe on 1,000 episodes covering all six orders: the head ignored the instruction.
    Real-arm success — 10 trials per checkpoint on the real arm, order red, blue, black, under the stack-bowls rollout protocol.

    Protocol

    1. Simulation: lerobot-nexarm-eval on seeds 1,000,000–1,000,019 (never used for training), 900 steps at most, --env.stack_order=red,blue,black.
    2. Real arm: the three bowls on the slots the simulation uses, all three cameras at 20 fps, the synchronous rollout engine.
    3. A real trial ends at success (all three stacked in order), at a collision, or after 90 seconds.
